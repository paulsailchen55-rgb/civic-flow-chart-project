# Civic Flow Chart Project

> An open, human-readable framework for mapping complicated civic processes—authority, responsibility, time, cost, friction, access, and renewal. First developed and tested in Santa Cruz County, California, with the goal of making public systems easier to understand, navigate, study, improve, and share anywhere.

## What this project is

The Civic Flow Chart is a proposed explanatory and navigation layer for public systems.

It maps how a civic process moves from one state or decision point to another, including:

- where a person or problem is located;
- what the person is trying to do;
- who has legal or administrative authority;
- who is responsible for the next action;
- what information or documents are required;
- what action occurs next;
- how long the step may take;
- what it costs;
- what happens if the step cannot be completed;
- how a person can pause, go back, appeal, or exit when those paths exist;
- how the process changes over time; and
- what record remains after the process is complete.

The project does **not** propose replacing government authority. Laws, regulations, official records, fees, deadlines, decisions, and operational instructions remain the responsibility of their authoritative sources.

The Civic Flow Chart is intended to make those systems easier to see and navigate.

## The basic routing question

**WHERE ARE YOU? → WHAT ARE YOU TRYING TO DO? → WHO HAS AUTHORITY? → WHO IS RESPONSIBLE FOR THE NEXT ACTION?**

This routing should work for an ordinary resident without requiring specialized civic knowledge.

## Child Test

A usable map should help a person answer:

1. What is happening?
2. What am I trying to do?
3. Where am I in the process?
4. Who decides?
5. Who is responsible for the next step?
6. What do I need to do?
7. Why is this required?
8. What does it cost?
9. How long should it take?
10. What if I cannot do it?
11. What happens next?
12. Can I go back, pause, appeal, or exit?
13. Where can I verify this information?

The Child Test is not a claim that civic processes are simple. It is a test of whether the explanation is understandable.

## Core node model

A Civic Flow node may contain:

**SPACE · TIME/CLOCK · PRIORITY · AUDIENCE · AUTHORITY · RESPONSIBILITY · INPUT · ACTION · STATUS · MONEY · NEXT · BACKTRACK · EXIT**

Not every node needs every field. Unknown information should be marked as unknown rather than guessed.

## Clock model

The project distinguishes different kinds of time instead of presenting one number as if it were a universal deadline:

- **Legal clock** — a time period established by law, regulation, order, or other authoritative rule.
- **Administrative target** — an agency or organization target or expected processing period.
- **Observed duration** — measured or reported time from actual cases.
- **Resident elapsed time** — the time experienced by the person navigating the process.
- **Financial clock** — a deadline or time pressure created by money, billing, financing, penalties, or other economic conditions.

A mapped clock should identify its source, starting point, expected duration or deadline, and consequences of delay when those are known.

## Friction

The project asks:

> **What is this friction doing?**

before asking:

> **What do I need to do?**

Friction may serve a legitimate purpose such as safety, environmental protection, public participation, rights, accessibility, or accountability. It may also accumulate through handoffs, duplicate requirements, unclear information, waiting, cost, distance, technology, or uncertainty.

The purpose of mapping friction is to make its function and cost inspectable—not to assume that all friction is unnecessary.

## Backtrack and reversibility

Navigation backtracking and legal reversibility are different.

A person should be able to navigate backward through the map even when the underlying legal or physical process cannot be reversed. Where a real appeal, correction, reconsideration, cancellation, or exit exists, the map should show it.

## Current process vs. proposed alternative

The project keeps these separate:

**CURRENT PROCESS** — what the authoritative sources say happens now.

**PROPOSED ALTERNATIVE** — a possible change, experiment, redesign, or community proposal.

This separation helps prevent a proposed improvement from being mistaken for an existing rule.

## Fact, model, estimate, opinion

The map distinguishes:

**FACT → MODEL → ESTIMATE → OPINION**

A source record should identify what kind of information it provides. A scientific paper may provide evidence without itself creating government authority. A government webpage may explain a process without being the underlying legal authority. A historical record may document what happened without being current policy.

## Three views of the same system

The same underlying process can be presented through three views:

- **Resident Map** — What am I trying to do?
- **Government Map** — Who is responsible?
- **Flow Map** — How does the process move?

These are views of one graph, not three separate systems.

## Twelve master doors

The first-level interface can use twelve broad categories. These are navigation categories, not a claim that government has only twelve parts:

1. Home, Land & Building
2. Money, Taxes & Economic Security
3. Health & Human Services
4. Roads, Transportation & Public Space
5. Water, Waste & Environment
6. Safety & Emergencies
7. Animals & Agriculture
8. Records, Information & Identity
9. Civic Participation
10. Justice, Rights & Accountability
11. Work, Business & Procurement
12. Government IT & Digital Access

## Emergency view

Emergency use can present a different interface over the same underlying map:

**WHAT IS HAPPENING? → WHERE? → IS ANYONE IN IMMEDIATE DANGER? → WHAT NEEDS TO HAPPEN NOW?**

A proposed operational sequence is:

**LIFE/SAFETY → IMMEDIATE HAZARD → CRITICAL INFRASTRUCTURE → ESSENTIAL SERVICES → RECOVERY → NORMAL CIVIC BUSINESS**

This is a proposed operational sequence, not a moral ranking of people or civic activities.

## Ledger, Lab, and Archive

### Ledger

Records friction, observed clocks, evidence, failed handoffs, stakeholder costs, verification questions, and proposed changes.

A correction should be simple—for example: **“This looks wrong.”**

The ledger itself must not become a new bureaucracy that creates more friction than it removes.

### Lab

A place to test alternatives and simulations by changing assumptions, authority, clocks, cost, or routing.

### Archive

Preserves prior versions, source records, plans, decisions, assumptions, and superseded pathways so current maps do not erase institutional memory.

A useful shorthand is:

**LIVING MAP → LEDGER → ARCHIVE → FUTURE**

## Civic memory, continuity, and renewal

The project distinguishes three related functions:

- **Civic Memory** — preserving records and accounts of what existed, what was attempted, what happened, and what was learned.
- **Civic Continuity** — carrying useful knowledge, relationships, resources, and accomplishments across periods of change.
- **Civic Renewal** — deliberately reviewing inherited systems and deciding what should continue, change, be replaced, be retired, or be remembered.

The lifecycle is:

**ALPHA → LIFE → OMEGA → REVIEW → RENEWAL → ALPHA′**

In secular public language, this can be described as a **Lifecycle Review** or **Renewal Cycle**.

> The purpose survives the version. The version does not become the purpose.

The Civic Flow Chart must contain its own exit: it should be possible to review, revise, replace, divide, consolidate, or retire the framework while preserving the historical record.

## Completion and contribution

The project distinguishes:

- **Official Record** — what an institution formally records.
- **Process Record** — how the system actually moved.
- **Experience Record** — what participants experienced.
- **Outcome Record** — what was accomplished.
- **Archive** — what remains available for later understanding.

A central question is:

> **Completion according to whom, for what purpose, and by what evidence?**

A civic system should record what it must, reveal what people need to navigate it, and leave room for human experience that cannot be completely represented by an official record.

Contribution is not ownership. Recognition is not authority.

## System gravity

**System gravity** is a working metaphor for the resistance a person or organization encounters when moving between civic states or nodes.

Possible components include time, money, distance, knowledge, physical access, technology, uncertainty, authority, and risk.

It is a design model, not a claim about a physical scientific force.

## Maintenance and uncertainty

Time-sensitive nodes need information such as:

- source owner;
- source location;
- date last verified;
- version or effective date;
- review trigger;
- responsible reviewer, where known.

The valid state **UNKNOWN / NOT YET MAPPED** is preferable to invented completeness.

A node may also be marked **DISPUTED** when relevant parties or sources disagree.

> The map does not have to settle every disagreement. It has to make the disagreement inspectable.

## Resident feedback and the evidence bridge

The Civic Flow Chart should not stop at navigation. Where appropriate, it should also provide a clear path for a resident's observation of a civic process to reach the organization responsible for operating, administering, reviewing, or making decisions about the relevant part of that system.

The basic loop is:

**PEOPLE REPORT → SYSTEM ROUTES → RESPONSIBLE PEOPLE REVIEW → AUTHORIZED ACTOR DECIDES → RECORD/RESPONSE → MAP UPDATES IF NEEDED**

The feedback bridge should make at least these stages visible:

- **Report** — what was observed or experienced, and where in the process it occurred;
- **Route** — which process, node, office, or organization is responsible;
- **Receipt** — confirmation that the report entered the intended channel, where practical;
- **Status** — what happened to it afterward;
- **Use** — whether it can appropriately contribute to aggregate evidence or system review.

### Experience and reflection

The system should preserve two different observation moments:

**IN-THE-MOMENT OBSERVATION** — what the person reported while experiencing the process.

**LATER REFLECTION** — what the person reported after having time to process the experience.

Neither should automatically overwrite the other or be treated as automatically superior. Immediate reports can be affected by emotion, fear, embarrassment, or time pressure. Later reflections can be affected by memory, social influence, incentives, or subsequent information.

The important design goal is to preserve provenance and context:

**EXPERIENCE → REFLECTION**

Emotion may be important evidence about a person's experience without automatically establishing an external fact. The system should distinguish what a person reported from what can be independently verified.

### Confirmation and traceability

Where technically and legally appropriate, confirmation can be provided by email, text message, printed receipt, or another accessible method. A confirmation may identify the receiving system, routing destination, receipt time, reference information, status, and correction path.

A receipt is not a promise that the report is correct, that the recipient agrees, or that the system will change.

### Authority and responsibility

The person or office receiving a report may not be the person authorized to change the underlying system. The map should distinguish, where applicable:

**REPORTER → RECEIVER → ROUTER → RESPONSIBLE OPERATOR → REVIEWER → AUTHORIZED DECISION-MAKER**

This prevents a generic complaint channel from being mistaken for decision-making authority.

### Individual reports and aggregate evidence

A single report may document an important individual experience. Multiple reports may reveal recurrence, location, timing, failed handoffs, or another pattern. Neither volume alone nor a single report automatically determines policy.

The evidence path should remain inspectable:

**OBSERVATION → REPORT → ROUTING → VERIFICATION → AGGREGATION → REVIEW/DECISION**

### Feedback is not the same as a survey

A standing feedback path and a formal survey can serve different purposes. A standing path can capture observations close to the time and place of actual interaction. A survey can deliberately ask a defined population a standardized set of questions later.

The Civic Flow Chart should preserve the provenance and conditions of each kind of evidence rather than assuming one mechanism is sufficient by itself.

### Privacy and aggregation

Public visibility and privacy are separate design questions. Where appropriate and lawful, individual case information can remain restricted while appropriately de-identified aggregate patterns are made available for civic understanding.

Possible classes are:

**PRIVATE CASE DATA → RESTRICTED INFORMATION → PUBLIC AGGREGATE → PUBLIC SOURCE EVIDENCE**

Anonymization by itself does not guarantee privacy. A production implementation requires appropriate privacy, security, records-management, retention, and legal review.

### Lightweight correction

A resident should be able to say:

> **THIS LOOKS WRONG.**

The system should be able to route the correction, preserve the prior version, and show the resulting status without requiring the resident to understand the entire architecture.

### Decision boundary

The feedback bridge routes and preserves evidence; it does not itself determine policy outcomes. Possible institutional responses include clarification, correction, operational adjustment, investigation, further study, referral, no change, or a proposal for formal action.

The bridge is intended to connect people and institutions, not replace authorized decision-making.

## Self-audit

The Civic Flow Chart could become exactly what it is trying to make visible.

Potential failure modes include:

- explaining authority and accidentally becoming authority;
- reducing friction while creating maintenance friction;
- showing a process and being mistaken for the process itself;
- simplifying complexity until important information disappears;
- preserving history until the archive becomes unusable;
- showing clocks that are mistaken for legal deadlines;
- showing costs that are mistaken for guaranteed prices;
- improving access while rewarding only sophisticated users;
- creating transparency that introduces a new gatekeeper;
- implying that government is more coherent than it really is;
- creating a correction process that becomes another bureaucracy;
- displaying choices in a way that appears to make the choices itself;
- turning feedback into a complaint warehouse with no usable routing or status;
- sending reports to the wrong authority without an easy correction path;
- exposing people through supposedly anonymous aggregation;
- making the feedback system a new prerequisite for obtaining service;
- overwhelming staff with duplicate, abusive, irrelevant, or unusable submissions;
- treating report volume as automatic proof of a particular policy conclusion.

The red-team questions include:

> **Who gets to decide that the Civic Flow Chart itself is correct?**

> **What happens if the Civic Flow Chart itself becomes friction?**

> **What happens if the feedback bridge becomes another wall between residents and the people responsible for the system?**

## Design principles

1. **Map first; change second.**
2. **Separate authority from explanation.**
3. **Show sources and uncertainty.**
4. **Distinguish legal deadlines from estimates and observations.**
5. **Make the next responsible actor visible.**
6. **Show available backtrack, review, appeal, and exit paths.**
7. **Preserve history without making history the current interface.**
8. **Work in print and basic HTML before requiring advanced technology.**
9. **Do not require AI, social media, or an account to understand a public process.**
10. **Design for people with little time, money, knowledge, technology, or institutional familiarity.**
11. **Keep proposed alternatives visibly separate from current rules.**
12. **Allow the framework itself to be reviewed, corrected, replaced, or retired.**
13. **Preserve both immediate experience and later reflection when collecting participant input.**
14. **Make feedback routing and receipt traceable without turning the feedback channel into a new bureaucracy.**

## Initial test environment

Santa Cruz County, California, is the initial test environment because it contains overlapping city, county, regional, state, and federal responsibilities and provides real processes through which the framework can be tested.

The framework is intended to remain portable. A Santa Cruz example should demonstrate the method, not define the method.

## Status

This repository is a public workbench. It contains a developing framework, not a claim of comprehensive coverage or official government endorsement.

Where a statement concerns a law, regulation, government procedure, fee, deadline, public record, or official decision, the authoritative source should be consulted and identified.

## Working rule

**Fast production. Slow release.**

Build quickly enough to explore ideas. Review slowly enough to avoid turning an interesting idea into an unexamined system.
