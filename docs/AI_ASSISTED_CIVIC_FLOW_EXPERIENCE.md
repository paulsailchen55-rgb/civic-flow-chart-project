# AI-Assisted Civic Flow Experience — Anonymized Case Study

**Date recorded:** 2026-09-17  
**Status:** Working research record  
**Privacy status:** Deliberately anonymized

## Purpose

This document records an experience that occurred while developing the Civic Flow Chart with AI assistance and then using the resulting work to contact several AI-related organizations.

It is included as a research observation, not as a complaint about any particular organization or person.

The purpose is to test whether the Civic Flow Chart's own concepts remain useful when the system being examined is partly digital, partly institutional, and partly AI-mediated.

## What happened

A public-interest proposal was developed around a practical closure principle:

> A system that creates a relationship, obligation, record, or dependency should provide a reasonably usable way to correct, transfer, complete, or end that relationship.

The proposal used five questions:

**SPACE → TIME → USE → RESPONSIBILITY → CLOSURE**

The proposal was sent to several AI-related organizations through publicly available contact channels.

The responses produced three different patterns:

1. **Closed intake / redirected responsibility** — one organization reported that a particular submission inbox had stopped accepting a class of submissions and explained that other matters should use different channels.
2. **Channel restriction / authenticated continuation** — another organization reported that the request would not continue by email and directed the sender into an authenticated account-based support process.
3. **Case creation / pending review** — another organization acknowledged receipt, assigned a case number, and stated that the request was under review.

No conclusion about the quality, legality, motive, or competence of any organization is intended by recording these events.

## Why this mattered to the project

The responses demonstrated that a modern institutional relationship can move through several distinct states:

**PERSON → CONTACT CHANNEL → CLASSIFICATION → ROUTING → AUTHENTICATION OR CASE CREATION → RESPONSIBLE TEAM → REVIEW → RESOLUTION / REDIRECTION / CLOSURE**

The important observation is not that these steps exist. Many are reasonable and necessary.

The observation is that a person can experience the transition between them as a change in the location, clock, responsibility, and rules governing the relationship.

That is exactly the kind of transition the Civic Flow Chart is intended to make visible.

## STUR observation

| Question | Observation |
|---|---|
| **SPACE** | The relationship may begin in email and move to an account, support portal, safety channel, or another designated system. |
| **TIME** | Some channels have closed intake periods, while other requests become pending cases or enter a separate processing clock. |
| **USE** | A single contact channel may distinguish among policy feedback, model-safety reports, security reports, child-safety matters, privacy matters, and ordinary support. |
| **RESPONSIBILITY** | The receiving inbox may not be the organization or team authorized to resolve the matter. |
| **CLOSURE** | A request may be closed, redirected, converted into a case, or required to move to another system. The practical question is whether the person can understand what state the relationship is actually in and what legitimate next or final step exists. |

## The deeper observation

AI did not create the institutional patterns observed here. Email routing, account authentication, ticketing, intake periods, departmental responsibility, and administrative handoffs all predate current AI systems.

AI can nevertheless make these patterns more consequential because AI can increase the speed, scale, persistence, and automation of information processing.

That raises a design question for AI-assisted civic systems:

> **When an AI-assisted system carries a person's request, record, obligation, or conflict forward, how does the system recognize that the underlying relationship has been resolved, redirected, expired, or legitimately ended?**

This is a question about lifecycle design, not about assigning blame.

## AI as participant, tool, and object of study

This project was developed using AI in several ways:

- AI helped organize research and ideas.
- AI helped translate complex concepts into simpler explanations.
- AI helped draft public-facing material.
- AI helped identify questions and possible process structures.
- The resulting proposal was then used in the real world to communicate with AI-related organizations.
- The responses became new observations that can be fed back into the design of the Civic Flow Chart.

This creates a loop:

**HUMAN OBSERVATION → AI-ASSISTED MODEL → PUBLIC ACTION → INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE → OBSERVATION → MODEL REVISION**

The loop should remain inspectable.

## No condemnation rule

This case study deliberately does **not** identify individual employees, private individuals, private email addresses, ticket numbers, or personal correspondence identifiers.

It also does not characterize any organization as good, bad, negligent, deceptive, or responsible for a problem merely because its process contains a particular handoff or restriction.

The purpose is to study the structure of the interaction.

Where an institutional response is recorded, it should be described as an observed response and separated from interpretation.

## Privacy and provenance

Anonymization is not assumed to guarantee privacy.

Before any future public release, the record should be checked for:

- names of individuals;
- personal email addresses;
- ticket or case identifiers;
- account identifiers;
- private correspondence;
- unnecessary quotations;
- unique details that could identify a person;
- information that could unfairly expose or condemn an individual.

The public version should retain enough information to understand the process while removing unnecessary personal identifiers.

## Design implications

The experience suggests several additions or tests for AI-assisted civic systems:

1. **Show channel boundaries.** Make clear when a request cannot continue through the current channel.
2. **Show routing responsibility.** Identify the next responsible system or actor when that information can be provided safely.
3. **Show state.** Distinguish received, redirected, pending, under review, resolved, closed, and unknown where applicable.
4. **Show the clock.** Distinguish an intake deadline from a processing estimate or an unresolved waiting period.
5. **Show closure.** Do not treat a redirect as equivalent to resolution.
6. **Preserve provenance.** Keep the original observation distinguishable from later summaries or AI-generated interpretations.
7. **Allow correction.** Provide a lightweight way to say that the mapped process is wrong or incomplete.
8. **Avoid mandatory AI dependence.** A person should not need an AI system merely to understand how to use or leave another system.
9. **Audit the auditor.** An AI-assisted map can itself become a new source of friction, error, persistence, or gatekeeping.
10. **Keep the human decision boundary visible.** AI may assist with routing, explanation, organization, or analysis without becoming the authority that decides the underlying civic question.

## Working conclusion

The experience did not prove that any particular institution has a closure problem.

It did demonstrate something more useful for this project: **the closure question can be applied to the communication channel itself.**

A person may ask:

> Where is my request now?
>
> What clock governs it?
>
> What is this channel for?
>
> Who is responsible for the next action?
>
> What happens when the request is finished, redirected, withdrawn, corrected, or closed?

Those questions can be asked without assuming that the underlying system is wrong.

That is the intended role of the Civic Flow Chart: make the relationship visible enough that people can understand it, question it, correct the map when necessary, and know when a process has actually ended.
