# Civic Flow Chart — Development Record & Worked Case Study

**Status:** Working reference / draft for review  
**Date:** September 2026

## Purpose

This document preserves the development of the Civic Flow Chart in a form that can be referenced from later work without requiring the entire working conversation to be reconstructed. It is not the authoritative specification and is not an official government document.

The concise **Version 1** describes the system. This document describes how the ideas developed and uses the 1214 Soquel Avenue / Seabright Plaza situation as a worked case study for testing the framework.

---

## 1. The problem that started the project

The central practical question was:

> **Can an ordinary person actually see and follow the path through a complicated civic process?**

The Civic Flow Chart is intended as an explanatory and navigation layer, not a replacement for government. Government institutions remain authoritative for their own laws, procedures, fees, deadlines, records, and decisions.

The basic routing became:

**WHERE ARE YOU? → WHAT ARE YOU TRYING TO DO? → WHO HAS AUTHORITY? → WHO IS RESPONSIBLE FOR THE NEXT ACTION?**

The goal is not to make complicated government falsely simple. The goal is to make the path through the complexity visible.

---

## 2. The project became a map of movement

The system is more than an organizational chart. It maps movement:

- a person moving through a process
- information moving between institutions
- responsibility moving between actors
- money and fees
- decisions
- deadlines
- appeals
- handoffs
- changes over time

Three complementary views developed:

| View | Basic question |
|---|---|
| Resident Map | What am I trying to do? |
| Government Map | Who is responsible / who has authority? |
| Transmission / Flow Map | How does the thing move? |

The Civic Flow grammar became:

**SPACE · TIME · PRIORITY · AUDIENCE · AUTHORITY · RESPONSIBILITY · INPUT · ACTION · STATUS · MONEY · NEXT · BACKTRACK · EXIT**

---

## 3. The clock

“How long does this take?” is not necessarily one question. The framework distinguishes:

- legal deadline
- administrative target
- observed/statistical duration
- resident's actual elapsed time
- financial deadline

It can also record when a clock starts, expected duration, waiting on the resident, waiting on government, actual duration, next deadline, and consequences of delay.

The purpose is to prevent a legal deadline, an agency target, and a person's lived waiting time from being treated as the same thing.

---

## 4. The child test

A recurring usability test became:

> **Could somebody who does not already understand the system figure out what is happening?**

Questions include:

- What is happening?
- What am I trying to do?
- Where am I?
- Who is responsible?
- Who decides?
- What do I do next?
- Why?
- How much does it cost?
- How long?
- What happens if something goes wrong?
- Can I go back?
- Can I pause?
- Can I appeal?
- Where can I verify this?

The test is not asking government to become simple. It asks whether the public-facing path is understandable.

---

## 5. Backtracking is not legal reversal

A Civic Flow Chart should allow a person to navigate backward through the map even when the underlying legal process does not allow an action to be reversed.

Therefore:

**Navigation backtracking ≠ legal reversibility.**

The process-state model developed as:

**BACKTRACK → PAUSE → CONTINUE → TRANSFER → APPEAL → RENEW → CLOSE**

An endpoint may be:

**END / TRANSITION / RENEWAL / TRANSFER / ONGOING**

Closure does not mean the history disappears.

---

## 6. Friction

The project changed the initial question from simply “Why is this difficult?” to:

> **What is this friction doing?**

A requirement may protect life safety, environmental resources, public participation, property rights, accessibility, financial accountability, or another legitimate interest. Friction can also accumulate through duplicate information, handoffs, waiting, distance, cost, technology, and uncertainty.

The method therefore does not automatically label friction as unnecessary. It asks what purpose the friction serves, what authority created it, what happens if it is skipped, and whether an alternative route exists.

---

## 7. System Gravity

**System Gravity** is a model/metaphor for the resistance a civic system presents to movement from one state or node to another. It is not a claim about a literal physical force.

Possible components include:

**time + money + distance + knowledge + physical access + technology + uncertainty + authority + risk**

**Experienced Gravity** describes the effective burden experienced by a particular participant attempting to traverse the same system.

This permits examination of accessibility while recognizing that a requirement may have a legitimate purpose.

---

## 8. The twelve doors

The first-level public interface developed twelve navigation categories:

1. Home / Land / Building
2. Money / Taxes / Economic Security
3. Health / Human Services
4. Roads / Transportation / Public Space
5. Water / Waste / Environment
6. Safety / Emergencies
7. Animals / Agriculture
8. Records / Information / Identity
9. Civic Participation
10. Justice / Rights / Accountability
11. Work / Business / Procurement
12. Government IT / Digital Access

Twelve is a first-level navigation limit, not a claim that government has only twelve components. The underlying graph can contain many more nodes.

---

## 9. Living Flow / Ledger / Archive / Lab

A major design problem was that putting all information into the current interface would make the interface itself difficult to use. The project therefore separated four layers:

**LIVING FLOW** — what a person needs now.

**LEDGER** — friction, observations, delays, actual clocks, failed handoffs, proposed changes.

**ARCHIVE** — historical versions and supporting records.

**LAB** — simulations and alternative models.

The purpose is to preserve historical complexity without forcing the current public interface to carry all of it.

This is also connected to the principle that information should survive the individual person who happened to gather it.

---

## 10. Information classification

The working distinction became:

**FACT → MODEL → ESTIMATE → OPINION**

Where useful, **CLAIM** identifies an assertion awaiting verification.

This distinction became especially important when the framework was tested against a live community situation. A community concern is not automatically a verified fact merely because someone reported it.

# Worked Case Study: 1214 Soquel Avenue / Seabright Plaza

## 11. Why this case was useful

The 1214 Soquel / Seabright situation provided a real-world test of whether the abstract framework could help people navigate a complicated local civic process.

The case should not be confused with the universal Civic Flow Chart. It is a test case.

The useful starting question is not “Is the project good or bad?” It is:

> **Can the process, evidence, authority, timing, and remaining choices be made visible enough that different people can examine them for themselves?**

---

## 12. What is currently proposed?

The City record identifies two SB330-only preapplications from Workbench:

- **CP26-0103:** six-story, 173-unit senior living facility with approximately 5,000 square feet of ground-floor commercial space.
- **CP26-0104:** six-story, 281-unit, 100% affordable housing project with approximately 5,000 square feet of ground-floor commercial space.

The City's material describes these as preliminary concepts rather than final project designs and states that no approval or denial decision is made by a preapplication.

The important Civic Flow questions are therefore:

- What is actually being proposed?
- What is preliminary?
- What is final?
- What stage is the process in?
- What has actually been decided?
- What remains open?

---

## 13. The housing process as a test flow

The housing test developed into:

1. **WHERE?** — City / County / special district / other jurisdiction.
2. **WHAT EXISTS?** — Parcel, existing use, zoning, General Plan designation, infrastructure, prior approvals, housing/community uses, relevant designations, and history.
3. **WHAT IS NEEDED?** — Homes, affordability levels, special needs, infrastructure, available land, and other documented needs.
4. **WHAT IS PROPOSED?** — Preserve, repair, convert, add units, replace, build new, increase density, change use, subsidize, regulate, acquire, relocate, or do nothing.
5. **WHAT CONSTRAINS IT?** — Governmental and nongovernmental constraints.
6. **WHAT PROTECTS SOMETHING?** — For each friction, identify its intended protection and authority.
7. **WHO DECIDES?** — Identify the applicable decision authority at the relevant stage.
8. **WHAT DOES IT COST?** — Fees, infrastructure, financing, carrying costs, subsidies, and other consequences.
9. **WHAT IS THE CLOCK?** — Legal deadline, administrative target, observed duration, actual elapsed time, financial deadline, and next deadline.
10. **WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?**

This turns a specific development controversy into a reusable process-testing grammar.

---

## 14. Community concerns versus verified facts

The case also demonstrated the need to separate listening from verification.

For example, a person might raise:

> “What happens to the existing businesses?”

The research record should not silently convert that concern into:

> “The project will eliminate the existing businesses.”

Instead, the concern becomes an investigable question:

> **What will happen to existing businesses and leases if the proposed project proceeds?**

The concern can remain part of the community record while the factual proposition remains unresolved until an appropriate source answers it.

---

## 15. Who answers versus who decides

A recurring discovery was that the person who can explain a process is not necessarily the person or body with authority to decide it.

Therefore the research record should contain separate fields for:

**Who can answer or verify this?**

and

**Who makes the decision, if different?**

This distinction prevents a helpful staff contact, applicant representative, community member, or researcher from accidentally being treated as the legal decision-maker.

---

## 16. The research audit

The case produced a second flow for checking the research itself:

**Listen → Record → Find the Source → Read → Check → Mark → Ask → Update**

A more compact version is:

**Question → Preliminary Answer → Source → Audit → Verified / Partly Verified / Unresolved**

The critical rule is:

> **Finding a source is not the same thing as checking the source.**

A source should be opened and the relevant material examined. The audit should ask whether the source actually supports the statement, only partially supports it, or does not support it.

“Unverified” is a legitimate result.

---

## 17. The community-question sandbox

The public sandbox is deliberately smaller than the full research record.

Its basic flow is:

**Question → Preliminary answer → Source → Check the source → Who can answer/verify? → Who makes the decision? → What remains unknown**

The experiment is:

**small example → people try it → identify confusion or missing pieces → revise → retest → expand**

The sandbox is therefore not intended to prove that the Civic Flow Chart is finished. It is a way to test whether the grammar is understandable to someone other than its creator.

---

## 18. Institutional memory and continuity

The development process also exposed a broader systems question: what happens when a knowledgeable participant becomes unavailable because of work, family, time, geography, or other responsibilities?

The design response is not to criticize that individual. It is to ask whether the information itself can survive the individual's departure.

> **The information should survive the individual.**

A public research record can preserve sources, dates, questions, corrections, and unresolved issues so that another person can continue without having to reconstruct the entire history.

---

## 19. Three layers of material

The work naturally separates into three layers:

### Private working material
Names, private conversations, strategy, raw notes, unverified allegations, internal disagreements, and other material not intended for public release.

### Research record
Questions, sources, verified facts, uncertainties, dates, official documents, and a visible audit trail.

### Public civic tool
The generic Civic Flow Chart framework, templates, process maps, methodology, and appropriately anonymized worked examples.

This separation allows a real community situation to inform development without automatically exposing private participants or turning a public civic tool into an advocacy record.

---

# 20. What the case taught the framework

The 1214 Soquel case helped expose several requirements for a usable Civic Flow Chart:

1. **Start with questions before conclusions.**
2. **Show the actual source.**
3. **Check the source rather than merely linking it.**
4. **Separate preliminary information from verified information.**
5. **Separate who explains from who decides.**
6. **Show where the process is in time.**
7. **Distinguish legal clocks from lived/observed duration.**
8. **Record what remains unknown.**
9. **Keep current rules separate from proposed alternatives.**
10. **Preserve history without forcing history into the current interface.**
11. **Make disagreement inspectable.**
12. **Allow the framework itself to be corrected.**

---

# 21. What this document is not

This development record is not:

- a legal opinion
- an official government document
- a statement of community consensus
- an architectural approval
- a prediction of a decision
- a replacement for authoritative government sources
- proof that every Civic Flow Chart concept has been tested

The 1214 Soquel / Seabright material is a **worked case study and test environment**.

---

# 22. Current working principle

The project can be reduced to two related principles:

> **Make the path from question to source to answer visible enough that another person can check it.**

and:

> **Make the path from error to correction visible and easy to follow.**

The larger design principle is:

> **A public system should be navigable by the people who are subject to it, not merely by the people who have already learned how to navigate it.**

The Civic Flow Chart is intended to equip people to understand and make their own decisions. It is not intended to make those decisions for them.
